The boy went to the market to buy rice.

Seven in ten ten-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries can’t read and understand a simple sentence like this one.1

We know how to fix it: find the level each child is actually at, and teach them from there. Teachers rarely get the time or support to do that for a class of fifty. Rung gives them a phone that does the measuring and planning, so they can do the teaching.

Why this hasn’t been solved yet

Grouping children by level instead of by grade, and spending an hour a day teaching each group at its level, is one of the most cost-effective ways known to raise learning. The World Bank’s evidence panel rates it a “great buy”.3

It stalls when it reaches ordinary government schools. Every child has to be assessed one by one, the class regrouped, a lesson planned for each group, and the teacher supported while they learn the method. Expert mentors are scarce and expensive, and teachers are already stretched.

That work is the part that has recently become cheap. Speech recognition can listen to a child read. Language models can turn results into a lesson plan and coach a teacher over a basic phone. In one World Bank trial in Nigeria, six weeks of AI-supported tutoring with a teacher in the room matched an estimated 1.5 to 2 years of ordinary schooling.4

How Rung works

  1. Check each child in a minute

    The child reads a short card aloud into the teacher’s phone. Rung listens and places them on a rung. A class of forty takes one period, and the check repeats every term.

  2. Group the class by level

    Rung sorts the class into groups and shows the teacher who sits where. Children move up as soon as they’re ready.

  3. Teach today’s lesson for each group

    Each morning the teacher gets a short, concrete plan for each group: games, cards and reading activities that need nothing beyond chalk and paper. Questions go to a coach that answers in the teacher’s language.

  4. Practise at home on a basic phone

    A short weekly voice call lets a child practise with a parent listening in. It works on any phone, with no data plan or app needed.

The reading check also catches some children who simply can’t see the print. They’re flagged for an eye test, because a child with poor vision learns about half as much as a child who can see clearly.5

What we promise about children’s voices

  • Recordings are scored on the phone where possible and deleted after scoring.
  • We store a child’s level, never their voice, face or full name.
  • Nothing is collected without a school’s agreement and a parent’s consent.
  • The tool and the methods are open source, and results are published whether they’re good or bad.

Where we are

Rung is pre-pilot. Nothing here has been tested with children yet, and the plan below is how we’ll find out whether it works.

  1. Late 2026Build the voice check in English and Hindi, then compare its scores with trained human assessors.
  2. Early 2027Add grouping, daily lessons and teacher coaching. Sign a school partner and get ethics approval.
  3. Spring 2027Pilot in about 20 schools, compared with 20 that don’t use Rung, over 50 teaching days.
  4. Late 2027Publish results and open-source the tool. Add languages and work toward adoption by a state school system.
